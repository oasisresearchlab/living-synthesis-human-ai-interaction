> **Example from industrial design.** Nuclear control rooms arrange meters into rows, columns, and smaller groupings such as 2 x 2 matrices. S15 argued this layout is not accidental: it is rooted in principles of visual perception, cognitive processing, and human factors design (pattern recognition, peripheral awareness, cognitive load management). (S15, SP-25)

> **Theory.** S15 added a theory summary, which they noted was an AI-generated review (so its citations should be checked):
> - *Parallel processing.* Vision is optimized for taking in and comparing many stimuli at once. In a regular grid, operators can use peripheral vision to spot inconsistencies without focusing on each meter (Wickens, 2008; Ware, 2019).
> - *Gestalt grouping.* Proximity and similarity make clustered meters (e.g., 2 x 2 matrices) read as a group, which reduces the cognitive effort of understanding relationships and supports pattern-based anomaly detection (Todorović, 2008).
> - *Pre-attentive processing.* Features like the angle of a needle are detected in under 200 ms, so a single needle pointing the wrong way stands out as a salient outlier (Healey & Enns, 2012).
> - *Spatial memory and chunking.* A regular grid supports a mental map of where indicators are, and chunking related meters reduces working memory demands (Endsley, 2018; Sweller, Ayres, & Kalyuga, 2011).
> - *Comparative scanning.* Perception excels at spotting relative differences between adjacent items in a regular structure, enabling relational processing in the spirit of ecological interface design (Vicente, 1999).
> - *So what.* Although digital interfaces have replaced analog control rooms, these principles remain relevant for modern visualizations and dashboards.
