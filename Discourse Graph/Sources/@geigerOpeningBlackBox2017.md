## Discussion notes
Claude:
> **Where it sat.** Listed among the design examples in the seminar readings, and assigned in Week 8 (March 13th, 2024) of the SP-24 seminar under "Collaboration / teaming / grounding". A student noted it seems to originate from Geiger's earlier paper "Open algorithmic systems: lessons on opening the black box from Wikipedia".

Claude:
> **Argument.** An ethnographic account of how bots and algorithmic tools shape Wikipedia's organizational culture. (Summarized from S13's notes, SP-24)
> - The opening vignette: a professor emailed Geiger asking how to correct misleading content on their own Wikipedia page. Best practice was to get at least one experienced Wikipedian to approve the requested changes, through an edit request process (account, talk page, templates) partly managed by bots like AnomieBot.
> - Wikipedians spend much of their time on "meta work" (reviewing, managing, coordinating, and policing contributors), and increasingly turn to automation and algorithmic systems for it. Geiger argues Wikipedia's success with few top-down structures is due less to the "wisdom of crowds" than to a "wisdom of bots, algorithmic agents, and smart interfaces that help structure the work of coordination behind the scenes".
> - Bots both facilitate editorial work and embody the community's values and norms. Instead of formality, rigidity, and consistency, "we find flux, revisability, and negotiation."
> - For veterans, bots are taken for granted: "some bot developers have told me that their bots work so seamlessly they are concerned other Wikipedians may not even know there is a bot automating various aspects of their workflow."

Claude:
> **Evidence on newcomers.** The paper offers grounds for [[CLM - algorithmic systems can raise the barrier to entry for newcomers to an organization because they can increase the complexity of organizational and governance practices that need to be learned in]] (S13's notes, SP-24):
> - "an overwhelming majority eventually give up because they are frustrated with both the social and technical learning curve required to participate as Wikipedians expect."
> - "by 2011, around 40% of newcomers making high-quality contributions had at least some of their edits reverted by fully automated bots or humans using semiautomated power tools, which had significantly contributed to the size of the editing community dropping from a peak of over 55,000 highly active editors per month in 2007 to a trough of under 35,000 highly active editors per month in 2012."
> - The key question it raises: "for whom are these sociotechnical systems negotiable and what do people have to do in order to exercise personal agency?" Understanding Wikipedia's governance takes not only technical knowledge (algorithmic or "trace" literacies) but also socialization into its culture.

Claude:
> **Critique of transparency.** Wikipedia's relatively open development and approval process for bots and code is a model worth studying, but "opening up the black box" is not a cure-all: "one of the paradoxes of openness is that it can take substantial time, energy, investment, and resources to fully take advantage of all the materials released" (citing Tkacz, 2015).

Claude:
> **Method.** Long-term participant-observation and virtual ethnography: 12 years as a volunteer Wikipedian (from 2004), formal ethnographic study from 2008, interviews, archival analysis of Wikipedia's governance and software history, computationally supported research, and hands-on bot development, including running a bot of his own.
> - S13 asked: when does a positionality and reflexivity statement become an autoethnography? (S13, SP-24)
